Chapter 3 — Demand

Demand is one of the most important concepts in microeconomics. It explains the relationship between the price of a commodity and the quantity that consumers are willing and able to buy.

Chapter Roadmap:
Meaning of Demand → Demand Schedule → Demand Curve → Law of Demand → Determinants → Individual Demand → Market Demand → Movement → Shift → Exceptions → Numericals

1. Meaning of Demand

Demand refers to the quantity of a commodity that a consumer is willing and able to purchase at different possible prices during a given period of time, other things remaining constant.

Three important elements of demand

  1. Desire to buy: The consumer wants the commodity.
  2. Ability to pay: The consumer has sufficient purchasing power.
  3. Willingness to pay: The consumer is actually prepared to buy.
Important: Desire alone is not demand. A person may desire an expensive car but if they cannot afford it, that desire is not effective demand.

2. Simple Example of Demand

Suppose the price of a notebook is ₹50. A student is willing and able to purchase 4 notebooks at this price. Therefore, 4 notebooks represent the quantity demanded at ₹50.

If the price falls to ₹40 and the student purchases 6 notebooks, quantity demanded has increased.

Price ↓ → Quantity Demanded generally ↑
Price ↑ → Quantity Demanded generally ↓

3. Demand Function

Demand function shows the functional relationship between quantity demanded and its determinants.

Qd = f(P, Y, Pr, T, E, N)
  • Qd = Quantity demanded
  • P = Price of the commodity
  • Y = Income of consumer
  • Pr = Prices of related goods
  • T = Tastes and preferences
  • E = Expectations
  • N = Number of consumers

6. Law of Demand

Other things remaining constant, quantity demanded of a commodity generally varies inversely with its price.

Price ↑ → Quantity Demanded ↓

Price ↓ → Quantity Demanded ↑

Assumption: Ceteris Paribus

The law of demand assumes that factors other than the price of the commodity remain unchanged.

Ceteris Paribus means "other things remaining constant."

8. Demand Curve

A demand curve is a graphical representation of the relationship between price and quantity demanded.

Price is normally shown on the vertical axis (Y-axis) and quantity demanded on the horizontal axis (X-axis).

Downward Sloping Demand Curve
The downward slope shows the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

9. Why Does Demand Curve Slope Downward?

  1. Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility: Additional units generally provide lower marginal utility.
  2. Income Effect: A fall in price increases the real purchasing power of consumers.
  3. Substitution Effect: A cheaper commodity becomes relatively more attractive compared with substitute goods.
  4. More Buyers: A lower price may make the commodity affordable to more consumers.

10. Determinants of Demand

1. Price of the Commodity

Other things remaining constant, a rise in price generally reduces quantity demanded.

2. Income of Consumer

For a normal good, an increase in income generally increases demand. For an inferior good, the relationship may be different.

3. Prices of Related Goods

  • Substitute goods: Tea and coffee.
  • Complementary goods: Car and petrol.

4. Tastes and Preferences

Changes in fashion, habits and preferences can change demand.

5. Expectations

Expectations about future prices or income can influence present demand.

6. Number of Consumers

A larger number of consumers can increase market demand.

7. Distribution of Income

Changes in the distribution of income can influence demand for different commodities.

11. Movement Along Demand Curve

When quantity demanded changes because of a change in the commodity's own price, it is called movement along the demand curve.

Two types

  • Expansion of Demand
  • Contraction of Demand
Movement Along the Same Demand Curve
Price falls → Expansion of demand.
Price rises → Contraction of demand.

The consumer moves from one point to another on the same demand curve.

12. Shift in Demand Curve

When demand changes because of factors other than the commodity's own price, the entire demand curve shifts.

Two types

  • Increase in Demand → Rightward Shift
  • Decrease in Demand → Leftward Shift
Increase and Decrease in Demand
A change in income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations, or number of consumers can shift the demand curve.

13. Movement vs Shift in Demand

Basis Movement Shift

14. Normal and Inferior Goods

Normal Good

A normal good is a good whose demand generally increases when consumer income increases, other things remaining constant.

Income ↑ → Demand for Normal Good ↑

Inferior Good

An inferior good is a good whose demand may decrease when consumer income increases, other things remaining constant.

Income ↑ → Demand for Inferior Good ↓

15. Substitute Goods

Substitute goods can be used in place of each other.

Examples

  • Tea and Coffee
  • Butter and Margarine
  • Bus and Train services in some situations
Price of Tea ↑ → Demand for Coffee may ↑

16. Complementary Goods

Complementary goods are goods that are generally consumed together.

Examples

  • Car and Petrol
  • Pen and Ink
  • Printer and Ink Cartridge
Price of Petrol ↑ → Demand for Cars may ↓

17. Exceptions to the Law of Demand

The law of demand is a general tendency and may not apply in certain special situations.

  • Giffen-type situations
  • Speculative demand
  • Prestige or Veblen-type goods
  • Expectation of future price increases
  • Emergency situations
These are special cases. The normal law of demand describes an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, assuming other factors remain constant.

18. Numerical Example

Suppose the price of a commodity falls from ₹10 to ₹8 and quantity demanded rises from 20 units to 25 units.

Question

Calculate the change in quantity demanded.

Change in Quantity = 25 − 20 = 5 units

Therefore, quantity demanded increases by 5 units.

19. Simple Demand Equation

A simple linear demand function may be written as:

Qd = a − bP
  • Qd = Quantity demanded
  • a = Constant term
  • b = Slope parameter
  • P = Price

Example

Qd = 100 − 5P

If P = ₹10:

Qd = 100 − 5(10)
Qd = 50 units

20. Connection with Elasticity
