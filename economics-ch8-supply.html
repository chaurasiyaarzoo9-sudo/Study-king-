Chapter 8 — Supply

Supply refers to the quantity of a commodity that a seller is willing and able to offer for sale at different prices during a given period of time.

Chapter Roadmap:
Supply → Stock vs Supply → Individual Supply → Market Supply → Law of Supply → Supply Schedule → Supply Curve → Determinants of Supply → Movement vs Shift → Elasticity of Supply → Formula → Numericals → Graphs → Exam Questions

1. Meaning of Supply

Supply is the quantity of a commodity that a producer or seller is willing and able to sell at a particular price during a given period of time.

Supply has four important elements:
  • Quantity of commodity
  • Willingness to sell
  • Ability to sell
  • Particular price and time period

2. Stock and Supply

Stock means the total quantity of a commodity available with the seller at a particular point of time.

Supply means the portion of stock that the seller is willing and able to offer for sale at a particular price.

Supply can be equal to or less than stock, but it cannot normally exceed the available stock.

3. Individual Supply

Individual supply refers to the quantity of a commodity supplied by a single seller at different prices during a given period.

Example

If one shopkeeper supplies 10 units at ₹20 and 15 units at ₹25, these quantities represent his individual supply at those prices.

4. Market Supply

Market supply refers to the total quantity supplied by all individual sellers in the market at different prices during a given period.

Market Supply = Sum of Individual Supplies

7. Law of Supply

The Law of Supply states that, other things remaining constant, quantity supplied of a commodity generally increases with a rise in its price and decreases with a fall in its price.

Price ↑ → Quantity Supplied ↑

Price ↓ → Quantity Supplied ↓
The law shows a direct relationship between price and quantity supplied, ceteris paribus.

8. Assumptions of Law of Supply

The Law of Supply operates when other relevant factors remain unchanged.

  • Prices of inputs remain constant.
  • Technology remains unchanged.
  • Prices of related goods remain unchanged.
  • Government policies remain unchanged.
  • Number of firms remains unchanged.
  • Future price expectations remain unchanged.
  • Natural conditions remain unchanged where relevant.
Ceteris Paribus means "other things remaining constant."

9. Supply Curve

A supply curve is a graphical representation of the relationship between price and quantity supplied.

The ordinary supply curve generally slopes upward from left to right.

Upward Sloping Supply Curve
The upward slope represents the direct relationship between price and quantity supplied.

10. Why Does Supply Curve Slope Upward?

  • Higher price can increase profitability.
  • Higher price can encourage producers to supply more.
  • Higher output may involve increasing marginal costs.
  • Existing firms may expand production.
  • New firms may enter a market when conditions permit.

11. Determinants of Supply

Supply is affected by factors other than the commodity's own price.

1. Price of Inputs

If input prices rise, production may become more expensive and supply may decrease, other things constant.

2. Technology

Improved technology can reduce production costs or increase productivity and may increase supply.

3. Prices of Related Goods

Producers may shift resources toward goods with relatively better returns.

4. Government Policy

Taxes and subsidies can affect production costs and incentives.

5. Number of Firms

An increase in the number of sellers can increase market supply, other things constant.

6. Future Price Expectations

Expectations about future prices can affect present supply.

7. Natural Conditions

Agricultural supply can be affected by rainfall, weather and other natural conditions.

12. Movement Along Supply Curve

A change in quantity supplied caused only by a change in the commodity's own price is called movement along the supply curve.

Expansion of Supply

When price rises, quantity supplied increases, causing an upward movement along the same supply curve.

Contraction of Supply

When price falls, quantity supplied decreases, causing a downward movement along the same supply curve.

13. Expansion and Contraction of Supply

Movement Along the Same Supply Curve
A → B represents expansion of supply when price rises. The curve itself does not shift.

14. Shift in Supply Curve

A shift in the supply curve occurs when supply changes due to factors other than the commodity's own price.

Increase in Supply

Supply increases due to favourable changes in determinants such as technology, input prices or government subsidies. The supply curve shifts to the right.

Decrease in Supply

Supply decreases due to unfavourable changes in determinants such as higher input costs or certain taxes. The supply curve shifts to the left.

15. Increase and Decrease in Supply

Shift of Supply Curve
A rightward shift from S₁ to S₂ represents an increase in supply.

17. Elasticity of Supply

Elasticity of supply measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the price of the commodity.

Es = Percentage Change in Quantity Supplied ÷ Percentage Change in Price
Es = (% ΔQ) ÷ (% ΔP)
Elasticity tells us how strongly quantity supplied responds to a change in price.

18. Types of Price Elasticity of Supply

1. Perfectly Inelastic Supply

Quantity supplied remains unchanged despite a change in price.

2. Relatively Inelastic Supply

Percentage change in quantity supplied is less than the percentage change in price.

3. Unitary Elastic Supply

Percentage change in quantity supplied equals the percentage change in price.

4. Relatively Elastic Supply

Percentage change in quantity supplied is greater than the percentage change in price.

5. Perfectly Elastic Supply

A very small change in price can be associated with a very large change in quantity supplied in the idealised case.

19. Elasticity of Supply — Formula

Es = (% Change in Quantity Supplied) ÷ (% Change in Price)

Percentage change can be calculated as:

% Change = Change ÷ Original Value × 100

20. Solved Numerical — Elasticity of Supply

Price increases from ₹10 to ₹12 and quantity supplied increases from 100 units to 120 units. Find elasticity of supply using the percentage method.

% ΔQ = (120 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100

= 20%
% ΔP = (12 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100

= 20%
Es = 20 ÷ 20

Es = 1
The supply is unit elastic in this numerical example.

21. Solved Numerical — Market Supply

At a price of ₹20, Seller A supplies 30 units and Seller B supplies 25 units. Find market supply.

Market Supply = 30 + 25

= 55 units

22. Solved Numerical — Percentage Change

Quantity supplied rises from 50 units to 70 units. Find percentage change in quantity supplied.

% Change = (70 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100

= 20 ÷ 50 × 100

= 40%

23. Important Factors Affecting Elasticity of Supply

  • Time period available to producers.
  • Ability to store the commodity.
  • Availability of inputs.
  • Mobility of factors of production.
  • Production technology.
  • Nature of the commodity.
Generally, producers have more opportunity to adjust supply over a longer time period than over a very short period.

25. Quick Concept Check

Q1. What happens to quantity supplied when price rises?

Answer: Generally, quantity supplied rises, other things constant.

Q2. What is market supply?

Answer: Sum of quantities supplied by all sellers in the market.

Q3. What causes movement along a supply curve?

Answer: Change in the commodity's own price.

Q4. What causes a shift in supply?

Answer: Changes in factors other than the commodity's own price.

Q5. What does elasticity of supply measure?

Answer: Responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.
