Chapter 10 — Market Forms

A market is a system through which buyers and sellers interact to exchange goods and services. Markets can be classified on the basis of the number of firms, nature of product, entry conditions, and degree of control over price.

Chapter Roadmap:
Market Forms → Perfect Competition → Monopoly → Monopolistic Competition → Oligopoly → Features → Comparison → Examples → Graphs → Revenue → Price Control → Exam Questions

1. Meaning of Market

In economics, a market does not necessarily mean a physical place. It refers to an arrangement through which buyers and sellers interact for the purchase and sale of a commodity or service.

A market may be physical, online, local, national or international.

2. Meaning of Market Form

Market form refers to the structure or organisation of a market. It depends mainly on factors such as:

  • Number of buyers and sellers
  • Nature of product
  • Freedom of entry and exit
  • Degree of competition
  • Control over price
  • Availability of information

3. Main Forms of Market

Market Form Basic Idea
Perfect Competition Many firms selling a homogeneous product
Monopoly Single seller
Monopolistic Competition Many firms selling differentiated products
Oligopoly Few dominant/interdependent firms

4. Perfect Competition

Perfect competition is an idealised market structure in which there are many buyers and sellers, the product is homogeneous, and individual firms cannot independently determine the market price.

Main Features

  • Large number of buyers and sellers
  • Homogeneous product
  • Free entry and exit in the idealised model
  • Perfect knowledge
  • High mobility of factors
  • Individual firm is a price taker
AR = MR = Price

5. Demand Curve Under Perfect Competition

For an individual competitive firm, the demand curve is horizontal at the market price in the standard model.

6. Monopoly

Monopoly is a market structure in which a single seller supplies a product or service to the market and close substitutes may be limited.

Features of Monopoly

  • Single seller
  • Large number of buyers may exist
  • Strong barriers to entry
  • No close substitute in the standard model
  • Firm has substantial control over price
  • Firm and industry are effectively the same production unit
Monopoly: One seller + barriers to entry + substantial market power.

7. Sources of Monopoly Power

  • Legal restrictions or exclusive rights
  • Control over an essential input
  • Large economies of scale
  • High barriers to entry
  • Network effects in some markets
  • Patents or other intellectual property rights for limited periods
Monopoly does not simply mean that a firm is large. The key idea is substantial control over supply and barriers to entry.

8. Monopoly Demand Curve

A monopolist faces the market demand curve. The demand curve normally slopes downward, meaning that to sell more output the firm generally needs to reduce price, other things remaining equal.

9. Monopolistic Competition

Monopolistic competition is a market structure in which many firms sell products that are similar but differentiated in some respects.

Main Features

  • Large number of sellers
  • Product differentiation
  • Some degree of price control
  • Relatively easy entry and exit compared with monopoly
  • Competition through price and non-price methods

Examples

Examples can include markets with many brands of clothing, restaurants, personal-care products, or similar consumer goods, where products differ in design, quality, location, service or branding.

10. Product Differentiation

Product differentiation means making a product appear different from competing products through characteristics such as:

  • Design
  • Quality
  • Packaging
  • Branding
  • Location
  • Customer service
  • Advertising
The products are substitutes, but consumers may perceive differences between brands.

11. Oligopoly

Oligopoly is a market structure in which a small number of firms account for a significant share of market supply.

Main Features

  • Few major firms
  • High degree of interdependence
  • Strategic decision-making
  • Entry barriers may be significant
  • Products may be homogeneous or differentiated
  • Price and non-price competition may occur
Key word: Interdependence. Each major firm's decisions can affect the others.

12. Homogeneous and Differentiated Oligopoly

Homogeneous Oligopoly

Firms sell products that are very similar or identical in relevant characteristics.

Differentiated Oligopoly

Firms sell products that are differentiated through branding, quality, features or other characteristics.

13. Comparison of Market Forms

Feature Perfect Competition Monopoly Monopolistic Competition Oligopoly
Number of Firms Very large One Large Few
Product Homogeneous No close substitute in standard model Differentiated Homogeneous or differentiated
Entry Free in idealised model Strong barriers Relatively easy Often difficult
Price Control Very little for individual firm Substantial Some Substantial but interdependent
Interdependence Very low Not applicable between competing firms Limited High

15. Revenue Concepts

Revenue is the income received by a firm from selling its output.

TR = P × Q
AR = TR ÷ Q
MR = Change in TR ÷ Change in Q
Concept Meaning Formula
Total Revenue Total receipts from sales TR = P × Q
Average Revenue Revenue per unit AR = TR/Q
Marginal Revenue Additional revenue from an additional unit MR = ΔTR/ΔQ

17. Price Discrimination — Basic Meaning

Price discrimination refers to charging different prices for the same or substantially similar product to different buyers or market segments when the differences are not fully explained by differences in costs.

Price discrimination requires market power and suitable conditions. It is mainly discussed in relation to monopoly or firms with market power.

18. Barriers to Entry

Barriers to entry are obstacles that make it difficult for new firms to enter a market.

  • Large capital requirements
  • Legal restrictions
  • Patents or exclusive rights
  • Control over important resources
  • Economies of scale
  • Strong brand loyalty
  • Network effects

19. Price Maker vs Price Taker

Price Taker Price Maker / Price Setter
Accepts market price Has some ability to influence price
Typical of perfect competition Associated with market power
Individual firm has little market power Firm has greater market power

20. Solved Numerical — Total Revenue

A firm sells 50 units at ₹20 per unit. Find total revenue.

TR = P × Q

= 20 × 50

TR = ₹1,000

21. Solved Numerical — Average Revenue

A firm's total revenue is ₹2,000 from selling 100 units. Find average revenue.

AR = TR ÷ Q

= 2000 ÷ 100

AR = ₹20

22. Solved Numerical — Marginal Revenue

Total revenue rises from ₹500 to ₹560 when output increases from 20 units to 21 units. Find marginal revenue.

MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ

= (560 − 500) ÷ (21 − 20)

MR = ₹60
